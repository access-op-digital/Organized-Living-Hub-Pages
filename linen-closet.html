Linen Closet

Modular, Built-In and Fixed Ventilated Wire Systems

Organized Living manufactures linen closet systems in three construction families: modular rail shelving that adjusts without tools, built-in wood systems finished like cabinetry, and fixed ventilated wire that brackets straight to the wall. Each system combines shelves, baskets, drawers and rods to hold folded towels, sheet sets and bathroom supplies inside the 15 to 24 inches of depth a standard linen closet allows. The company has manufactured storage systems since 1919 and builds them at a 680,000 square foot facility in Bloomington, Indiana, where the load ratings are tested: Lifetime ventilated wire carries up to 75 pounds per linear foot and Shelftech up to 45. Those load figures, the finish specifications and the warranty terms are all published on this site, and orders ship across the United States and Canada in 3 to 5 business days.

Types of Linen Closet Systems

Three construction families cover linen storage, and the family you choose decides how the closet behaves over the years rather than only how it looks on day one. A modular system hangs its shelves from a rail and moves whenever the contents change. A built-in system fixes shelves and drawers in position for a cabinet finish. Fixed ventilated wire brackets to the wall and keeps air moving through every stack.

Linen Closet Shelving and Components

Four component groups assemble into a working system: shelves, baskets, drawers, and the hooks and rods that claim leftover vertical space. Choosing components rather than a fixed cabinet is what lets one closet hold towels at the front, backstock above and a hamper below, and closet organizers from this range mix freely across all three construction families.

Ventilated Wire Shelves

Air moves through a ventilated wire shelf instead of stopping against a solid panel, which is why these shelves carry the main linen load. Damp bath towels and stored bedding both benefit, because a closed linen closet generates no airflow of its own.

Pull-Out Baskets

Pull-out baskets turn the unreachable back half of a deep shelf into usable storage. Anything past roughly 16 inches disappears behind whatever stands in front of it, and a basket that slides forward is the difference between storing washcloths and burying them.

Drawers and Soft-Close Glides

Small items that refuse to stack belong in drawers: face cloths, travel bottles, medicine and dressings. Soft-close glides stop a loaded drawer slamming in a closet that often shares a wall with a bedroom.

Why Choose Organized Living for Your Linen Closet

Four of the five reasons below are specific to linen rather than to closets generally, because folded linen loads a shelf differently from anything else a household stores.

Rated for Stacked Linen Weight

Folded linen loads a shelf harder per foot than hanging clothes, and the published ratings are sized for exactly that: 75 pounds per linear foot on Lifetime ventilated wire, 45 on Shelftech. Both figures come from testing rather than from the design drawing, and both are published on the product pages.

Airflow Around Stored Linens

Ventilated wire moves air around towels and bedding inside a closed closet, which a solid shelf cannot manage. That is why wire remains standard in linen storage long after it has been replaced elsewhere in the house.

Adjustable Shelf Spacing

Sheet sets, folded towels and bulky duvets each need a different clearance, and a rail system holds all three without settling on a compromise height. Spacing changes as the contents change, with no new holes in the wall.

Built for Shallow and Narrow Closets

Linen closets are rarely walk-ins, and the range is built for the depths they actually are. Shelf and bracket sizes cover the shallow hallway closet and the narrow run instead of assuming the dimensions of a wardrobe.

Manufactured in Bloomington Since 1919

We have built storage systems since 1919 and remain third-generation family owned. Production runs from a 680,000 square foot facility in Bloomington, Indiana, which is also where the testing happens.

Spacing follows the contents, which is why bulky bedding belongs on the highest or lowest shelf rather than the middle of a run. Put it mid-wall and it forces every shelf above it wider than the towels need.

Depth is the figure worth getting right. Past roughly 20 inches the back of a shelf stops being reachable, and that is where a pull-out basket earns its cost instead of a deeper shelf.
